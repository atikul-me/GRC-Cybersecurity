# 📊 NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 — CSF Profiles and Tiers

> **Reference:** NIST CSWP 29 (February 26, 2024)  
> **Topic:** Section 3 - Introduction to CSF Profiles and Tiers

---

## 🧩 3.1. CSF Profiles

A **CSF Organizational Profile** describes an organization’s **current and/or target** cybersecurity posture in terms of the CSF Core's outcomes. 

### 🎯 Purpose of Organizational Profiles
Organizational Profiles are used to **understand, tailor, assess, prioritize, and communicate** the Core’s outcomes by considering an organization's:
* **Mission objectives**
* **Stakeholder expectations**
* **Threat landscape**
* **Requirements**

### 📋 Types of Profiles
Every Organizational Profile includes one or both of the following:

1. **Current Profile:** Specifies the Core outcomes that an organization is **currently achieving** (or attempting to achieve) and characterizes *how* or *to what extent* each outcome is being achieved.
2. **Target Profile:** Specifies the **desired outcomes** that an organization has selected and prioritized for achieving its cybersecurity risk management objectives. It considers anticipated changes such as new requirements, new technology adoption, and threat intelligence trends.

> 💡 **Community Profile:**  
> A **baseline** of CSF outcomes created and published to address shared interests and goals among multiple organizations (e.g., for a specific sector, subsector, technology, threat type, or use case). An organization can use a **Community Profile as the basis for its own Target Profile**.

## 🔄 Steps for Creating and Using a CSF Organizational Profile

To inform continuous improvement of its cybersecurity posture, an organization can follow this 5-step iterative cycle:

```
[ 1. Scope the Organizational Profile ]
                  │
                  ▼
[ 2. Gather needed information ]
                  │
                  ▼
[ 3. Create the Organizational Profile ]
                  │
                  ▼
[ 4. Analyze gaps and create an action plan ]
                  │
                  ▼
[ 5. Implement action plan and update Profile ] ──► (Repeat as needed...)
```

### Step-by-Step Breakdown:

1. **Scope the Organizational Profile:**  
   Document the **high-level facts and assumptions** on which the Profile will be based. An organization can have multiple Organizational Profiles, each with a **different scope** (e.g., covering the entire organization, specific financial systems, or countering ransomware threats).

2. **Gather the Needed Information:**  
   Collect relevant internal data, such as:
   * Organizational policies, risk management priorities, and resources
   * Enterprise risk profiles and Business Impact Analysis (BIA) registers
   * Cybersecurity requirements and standards
   * Practices, tools (procedures and safeguards), and work roles

3. **Create the Organizational Profile:**  
   Determine what types of information to include for selected CSF outcomes and **document** it. Consider the risk implications of the **Current Profile** to inform Target Profile planning, and consider using a **Community Profile** as a baseline.

4. **Analyze Gaps and Create an Action Plan:**  
   Conduct a **gap analysis** to identify and analyze the differences between the Current and Target Profiles. Develop a prioritized action plan—such as a **risk register**, **risk detail report**, or **Plan of Action and Milestones (POA&M)**—to address those gaps.

5. **Implement the Action Plan and Update the Profile:**  
   Follow the **action plan** (which may have an overall deadline or be ongoing) to close gaps and move toward the **Target Profile**. Repeat these steps as often as needed for **continual improvement**.

### 🤝 Additional Uses for External Stakeholders
* **Current Profile:** Can be used to document and communicate cybersecurity capabilities and known improvement opportunities with external stakeholders (e.g., business partners or prospective customers).
* **Target Profile:** Can express cybersecurity risk management requirements and expectations to suppliers, partners, and third parties.

## 📈 3.2. CSF Tiers

An organization can choose to use **CSF Tiers** to **inform** its Current and Target Profiles. Tiers characterize the **rigor** of an organization’s cybersecurity risk governance and management practices, providing context on how the organization views risks and the processes in place to manage them.

### Progression of CSF Tiers (Fig. 4)

```
                                              ┌──────────────────┐
                                              │      Tier 4      │
                                              │     Adaptive     │
                             ┌────────────────┴──────────────────┤
                             │      Tier 3                       │
                             │    Repeatable                     │
            ┌────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┤
            │      Tier 2                                        │
            │  Risk-Informed                                     │
┌───────────┴────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Tier 1                                                        │
│  Partial                                                       │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

* **Tier 1: Partial** — Informal, ad hoc responses to cybersecurity risk.
* **Tier 2: Risk-Informed** — Risk management practices are approved by management, though not necessarily established as organization-wide policy.
* **Tier 3: Repeatable** — Formalized, regularly updated risk management practices applied consistently across the organization.
* **Tier 4: Adaptive** — Agile, risk-informed, and continuously improving approaches adapting to evolving threats and technologies.

### Key Takeaways on Tiers:
* **Sets the Overall Tone:** Selecting Tiers helps set the **overall tone** for how an organization manages cybersecurity risks.
* **Complements Methodology:** Tiers should *complement*—not replace—an organization's existing cybersecurity risk management methodology (e.g., serving as an internal benchmark).
* **When to Advance:** Progression to higher Tiers is encouraged when **risks or mandates are greater**, or when a **cost-benefit analysis** indicates a feasible and cost-effective reduction of negative cybersecurity risks.
